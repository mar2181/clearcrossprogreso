# Advertising ClearCross — what the rules actually key on

Researched 2026-09-07, in answer to a direct question: *"is there a difference if its
origin is in Mexico or the United States as far as any rules... if it's safe to put it
out as if it's a Mexican website we can do that... the businesses are in Mexico so
there's different rules in Mexico."*

⛔ **This is research, not legal advice.** Nothing here replaces the Texas healthcare
attorney the go-to-market plan already gates the commission model on. What it does is
settle which questions actually need one.

---

## The short answer

**Presenting the site as Mexican would not remove a single restriction, and it would
create three new ones.** Every rule that could bite us keys on **who the audience is**
and **who the advertiser is** — not on where the server sits, what the TLD says, or
where the clinics are.

⭐ **And the good news is bigger than the bad news:** the two things this business
actually wants to do — advertise a **directory of dental and eye care**, and charge a
**flat listing fee** — are both squarely permitted, and the flat fee is *expressly*
carved out by the Texas rule everyone assumes forbids it. See sections 4 and 5.

---

## 1. Why "make it a Mexican website" fails — three independent reasons

### 1a. The ad platforms do not look at the website's nationality

Google's healthcare policy is organised as a **location-by-location table** and says
plainly: *"Check the location-specific restrictions for every location where you plan
to advertise"* and *"You're expected to familiarize yourself with the local law and
regulations for any location your ads target."*

⇒ the gate is the **targeted audience**. We target the Rio Grande Valley, so we get the
United States rules whatever the domain says. A `.mx` domain, Mexican hosting and a
Spanish-first homepage would change **nothing** about which policy applies.

### 1b. Obscuring who we are is itself the violation, and the penalty is terminal

Google's Misrepresentation policy: *"Google doesn't allow making misleading statements,
obscuring, or omitting material information about your identity, affiliations, or
qualifications."*

And the enforcement, verbatim: *"your Google Ads accounts will be suspended upon
detection and without prior warning, and you will not be allowed to advertise with
Google Ads again."*

⛔ **No warning, no appeal path, and it is not scoped to one ad.** Google reviews *"your
ad, website, accounts, and third-party sources."* Advertiser verification then publishes
the advertiser's real **name and location** on every ad in the Ads Transparency Center —
so the identity is disclosed by the platform regardless, and the only thing a false
origin adds is the discrepancy that triggers the ban.

🔴 **This is what makes it a bad trade for the whole business, not just this site.** The
agency runs six client Facebook pages and their ad accounts through shared
infrastructure. A misrepresentation suspension is the one failure mode that can reach
past the offending property.

### 1c. There is a direct enforcement precedent, and it is this idea in mirror image

**FTC v. Karnani / Best Priced Brands (2011).** A **California** company sold to British
consumers through `.co.uk` domain names, so buyers believed they were dealing with a UK
retailer. The FTC brought it under the **U.S. SAFE WEB Act**, and the settlement order
specifically banned the defendants from misrepresenting *"the location"* of their
business. $500,000 judgment.

⇒ dressing a domestic operation in a foreign national costume, in front of consumers a
regulator cares about, is a fact pattern the FTC has already litigated and won.

⚠️ And SAFE WEB cuts **both** ways: it *"expressly affirms the FTC's authority to pursue
deceptive or unfair acts or practices involving foreign actors targeting Americans."* So
even a genuinely Mexican entity advertising to Texans is inside FTC reach. There is no
offshore door here — the Act was written to close it.

## 2. What the platforms actually gate on

| gate | keys on | our position |
|---|---|---|
| Which healthcare rules apply | **audience location** | US / Texas, always |
| Certification (LegitScript) | **what you do** — dispense, prescribe, sell | ⭐ we do none of the three |
| Advertiser identity | **the billing entity** | a Texas company; disclosed by Google anyway |
| Ad copy claims | **the words in the ad** | our guard covers the site, **not the ads** |

⭐ **The single most valuable fact in this research pass:** LegitScript certification and
Meta's pharmacy authorization attach to **online pharmacies, telehealth providers and
drug manufacturers**. ClearCross is none of them. We do not dispense, prescribe, ship,
or take payment for care. We are a **directory and a publisher**.

⛔ **That posture is worth protecting and is easy to lose.** The moment the site books
appointments, takes a deposit, or fulfils anything, it stops being a publisher and
becomes a party to the transaction — and every certification question opens at once.
Keep the transaction between the patient and the clinic.

## 3. What we can and cannot advertise — by category

This is the actionable part. The categories differ far more than they look.

### 🟢 Dental, optical, spa — advertise freely

An ad reading *"Compare dentist prices in Nuevo Progreso — 17 clinics, real published
prices"* promotes a **directory**, not a treatment. No certification gate, no
prescription rules. It is also, separately, where the money is: dental is the category
with real price depth.

### 🟡 The guide and logistics content — advertise freely, and prefer it

Bridge tolls, parking, wait times, what you may bring back. Zero healthcare policy
surface, it is the SERP field we are already winning, and it is honest top-of-funnel for
everything else on the site.

### 🔴 Pharmacy and prescription prices — do NOT advertise, and the reason is not the platform

This is the finding I did not expect, and it is the real constraint.

The FDA's personal importation policy is far narrower than the popular understanding. In
FDA's own framing it applies where *"effective treatment is not available in the United
States"*, for *"a serious medical condition"*, and — decisively — **"FDA intended this
policy to allow individuals to gain access to treatments not otherwise available in the
United States"** and *"has normally not been intended as a way for consumers to bring
lower-priced prescription drugs into the United States."*

⇒ **"cheaper meds across the border" is, in FDA's own words, the thing the policy is not
for.** Doing it is generally tolerated as enforcement discretion; **advertising it** is a
different act with a different audience — a regulator reading our ad, not a CBP officer
reading a traveller's declaration.

⛔ Layered on top: Meta gates prescription-drug ads behind authorization and
LegitScript-equivalent verification, and Google restricts *"the online prescribing,
dispensing, and sale of prescription drugs."* Even if we cleared FDA comfort, we would be
applying for certifications built for pharmacies, as a company that is not one.

**So: the pharmacy pages stay on the site as reference and SEO** — publishing a price
somebody else published is not the same act as buying an audience for it — **and no ad
dollar points at them.** They will still rank. They just will not be promoted.

⚠️ Recorded honestly: `content/blog/what-you-can-bring-back-from-nuevo-progreso.mdx` is a
legitimate informational page and is arguably our most useful one. It is fine where it
is. **Do not put paid media behind it** — the ad is what turns "we described the rules"
into "we solicited the conduct."

## 4. Texas — and the answer is better than the standing assumption

`STATE.md` has carried, correctly and cautiously, that the **Texas Patient Solicitation
Act** gates any per-patient commission. Reading the statute changes the picture in two
ways, one of them decisive.

**Tex. Occ. Code Sec. 102.001(a)** makes it an offence to pay or accept remuneration for
soliciting a patient *"for or from a person licensed, certified, or registered by **a
state health care regulatory agency**."*

⭐ **A dentist in Nuevo Progreso is licensed by Mexico** — a Cedula Profesional issued by
SEP — **not by any US state health care regulatory agency.** On the plain text, the
clinics we list are not the class of provider that section protects. ⛔ That is a
plain-text reading by someone who is not a lawyer, it is exactly the kind of question
where a prosecutor has other theories, and it stays on the attorney's list. But it means
the commission model is **an open question, not a closed door**, which is a different
thing from what we had written down.

⭐ **And the flat fee needs no argument at all.** The Texas dental board rule
(22 TAC 108.58) expressly permits *"remuneration for advertising, marketing, or other
services that are provided for the purpose of securing or soliciting patients"* where it
is:

1. *"set in advance"*
2. *"consistent with the fair market value of the services"*
3. *"not based on the volume or value of any patient referrals"*

⇒ **a flat monthly listing fee is the carve-out, written out.** It is not a workaround or
a lesser option — it is the arrangement the rule contemplates. Price it in advance, price
it at market, and never tie it to leads delivered.

**Which settles the sequencing the go-to-market plan already proposed, on the law rather
than on caution:** flat listing fee now, commission only after counsel.

---

## 5. What to do

1. **Advertise as exactly what we are** — a Texas company publishing a price directory
   for a Mexican border town. It is the truth, it is a good story, and it is the only
   posture that survives contact with Google's own transparency page.
2. **Point ad spend at dental, optical and the logistics guides.** Never at pharmacy or
   prescription pricing.
3. **Complete Google advertiser verification** before the first campaign, with the real
   entity. The name and location get published either way.
4. **Geo-target the Valley** — which is where the audience is regardless.
5. ⛔ **Ad copy needs its own guard.** `test/honest-claims.mjs` sweeps the site; an ad
   headline is written in a platform console and never touches this repo. Every rule in
   `docs/HONEST_CLAIMS.md` applies to ad copy and **nothing currently enforces it there**.
   The likeliest violation is an ad implying we verify clinics.
6. **Keep the transaction out of the site** (section 2).

⚠️ Standing decision, unchanged: **$0 ad spend, organic only**, until the measurement is
real. Nothing above is a recommendation to start spending — it is the answer to what
happens when we do.
